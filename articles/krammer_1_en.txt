<h2 class="subtitle">Introduction</h2>

For years and decades, we have been threatened with the spectre of "terrorism", even though the measures taken against it, such as the "war on terror", often seem more frightening than terrorism itself. Especially when the mainstream media talks about Palestine or the so-called Middle East, the term "terrorism" is used conspicuously often, sometimes already as a catchphrase: "Palestinian" or "Arab", or at least "Islamic" terrorism, in order to portray a certain region of the world as a particularly haunted and dangerous place - not least because of a religion that is particularly widespread in the global South. It is probably no coincidence that these are mostly countries that are also among Israel's declared enemies and/or claimed territories.

Undoubtedly, and as confirmed by journalists affected, several media companies require their editors to adhere to specific wording and framing, meaning that organisations such as Hamas or Hezbollah must never be mentioned without the additional terms "terrorist organisation", "terrorist group" or "terrorist militia", as if these were their original names, like a person's first and last name. It is rather striking when this labelling is neglected in the mainstream media, just as, conversely, any term referring to the Israeli government or army must, of course, be used without any additional negative designation (such as "occupying power" or "right-wing extremist").

Less well known or often concealed is the fact that even before the state was founded, Zionism relied on methods that, even by Western standards, must be classified as typical of classic terrorism, and continues to use them to this day. Strictly speaking, Israel owes its existence to terrorism, because it is simply a fact that the Israeli army was recruited from gangs wanted by the police. In this text, I would like to explain this assertion, which some may find outrageous, in more detail.

<h2 class="subtitle">The label of terrorism</h2>

In any case, it is rare for someone to describe themselves as a terrorist; it is almost exclusively a label applied by others. It is also no secret that the term "terrorism" suffers from political ambiguity, and not only because the resistance of the opposing side is presented as terrorism and the terrorism of one's own side as resistance, self-defence or a struggle for freedom (cf. Kocher 2011, 107). There is also a broad field of what actually falls under this category. In addition to the militancy that has been assumed up to now, there is now eco-terrorism, cyberterrorism and, according to the Turkish AKP government, the new term of unarmed terrorism, which allows the regime to imprison unpopular intellectuals, teachers, artists and opposition politicians (Laciner 2016). However, we are also familiar with this from Western European countries such as Germany and Austria, where the alleged or actual approval of terrorism is already considered as such, and recently, one or two slogans interpreted as containing some kind of secret code that could threaten the right of Israeli settler colonialism to exist or its aspiration to carry out genocide have been sufficient to constitute terrorism (cf. Colla 2023). Whether state terrorism can also be named as such is controversially discussed within international institutions and rejected above all by Western states (at least as long as it does not involve accusations against those demonised governments and candidates for regime change who have become the focus of imperialist interventionism).

Resistance to occupation, which is clearly permitted under international law but mostly defamed as terrorism, is in any case only granted to pro-Western mercenaries, such as the neo-Nazis of Azov or the paramilitaries of the allied UCK during NATO's war of aggression against Yugoslavia. Furthermore, Israel, of all countries, is granted a right of self-defence against the occupied, as if ethnic cleansing and genocide were a form of resistance against terrorist hordes, thereby reducing all international regulations to absurdity. In the Hasbara Handbook and similar guides (cf. Brynin 2023; World Union for Jewish Students 2002), Israel's defenders are instructed to legitimise all actions of the Zionist state by claiming that they are merely a response to terrorism, just as it was radical Zionist neoconservatives who, at the turn of the millennium, proclaimed the aforementioned "war on terror", using this slogan to bomb several southern countries to rubble and ashes and murder millions of people.

<h2 class="subtitle">Competition among Zionist terrorist groups</h2>

It was clear to the Zionist masterminds that the Palestinians would not voluntarily move to neighbouring Arab countries and that "gradual expropriation" was a myth that no settler had really believed in since Theodor Herzl's time (cf. Herzl 1922, 98). From the outset, therefore, the Zionists saw terrorism as a suitable means of driving the indigenous population out of Palestine and replacing the British Mandate with their own occupation.

To this end, the Zionist factions each founded their own military organisations. Hagana and Palmach were on the side of the Labour Party, while Etzel-Irgun Zwai Leumi and the even more right-wing splinter group Stern or Lehi were traditionally affiliated with the Revisionists and thus the later Likud. The Haganah was subordinate to the Jewish Agency and was also controlled                                                     by the trade union, specifically the Histadrut, founded in 1920. It was therefore closer to the Zionist representation to the outside world and, at least until the end of the Second World War, sought a more relaxed relationship with the British Mandate.

The Irgun, founded in 1931 by Jabotinsky, felt that all this was not happening quickly enough, especially after the 1939 White Paper proposed a two-state solution, while the Zionists claimed the whole of Palestine - "from the river to the sea" - (even though they did not even control 3% of the land at that time). To achieve this goal, the Irgun not only wanted to forcibly expel the Palestinian civilian population, but also to use armed force to compel the Mandate power to implement the Balfour Agreement more quickly (which, as is well known, had promised the creation of a separate Zionist state in a letter from British Foreign Secretary James Balfour to Lord Walter Rothschild in 1917). According to the Palestinians, the main enemy of the Irgun – as well as most other Zionist organisations – was Great Britain and not the Nazis, as later Zionist propaganda suggested (cf. Arendt 1991, 91). These settlers had nothing to do with the fight against anti-Semitism because they were exclusively concerned with the establishment of a Zionist state, for which they considered the ethnic cleansing of Palestine a necessary prerequisite and legitimate means. All other political concerns were subordinated to this main goal.

Incidentally, the victim of the first assassination carried out by the Haganah in 1924 was the Jewish journalist and writer Jacob Israel De Haan, who, after initially enthusiastic support for Zionism, had dared to speak out against this ideology and advocate a negotiated solution with the Palestinians (cf. Ilany 2024). This first fatality of Zionist terror shows that the fighting units were not concerned with anti-Semitism, but that it was precisely the "internal Jewish opponents," as the Zionists called them, who were the first to be targeted by the Haganah (Arendt 1991, 89). The terrorist who carried out the attack, Avraham Tehomi, later founded the Irgun (also known as IZL or Etzel, due to the abbreviation of Irgun Zwai Leumi [<i>"National Military Organisation"</i>]) together with Zeev Jabotinsky, which, as mentioned above, represented revisionist and even more right-wing political positions and attracted attention with sensational terrorist attacks (cf. Tamir 2012). Zeev Jabotinsky is a central figure because he played an important role in almost all armed Zionist groups, not just the extreme right. First a leading figure in the founding of the Jewish Legion, which was then integrated into the British Army, then in an important position in the Haganah and the World Zionist Organisation, he later became the leading integrating figure for revisionists and the Irgun.

When debates arose within the Irgun about the legitimacy of attacks on British institutions, a new terrorist organisation was formed in 1940. At that time, the even more nationalistic Lehi or Stern Gang (named after its founder Avraham Stern) split off but continued to cooperate with Menachem Begin's Irgun in some massacres, such as in Deir Yasin (cf. Brenner 1984).

Lehi also sought direct contact with the German fascists, which is why, on their initiative, a meeting took place in 1941 between the representative of the Stern Gang, Naftali Lubenczik, and Otto von Hentig, representative of Nazi Germany, in Beirut. The infamous letter to the Nazis, sent by Lehi<a href="#fn1" id="fn1back" class="backlink"><sup>i</sup></a> to the German embassy in Ankara in January 1941, proposing direct cooperation, is another historical document that reveals the Stern Gang's shameful attempts at collaboration (Krammer 2010, 108). To this day, historians disagree on whether and in what form this cooperation continued. Both Lubenczik and Stern were arrested by the British, and Stern himself was executed in 1942 (cf. Kushner 2003). Men like Yitzhak Shamir took his place and continued the terrorist attacks without it hindering their later political careers. Shamir, for example, was appointed to the Mossad after the founding of the State of Israel, before being appointed Foreign Minister and finally Prime Minister.

<h2 class="subtitle">The Haganah invites Eichmann to Palestine</h2>

But it was not only Lehi that showed a willingness to collaborate; the Haganah also sent its agents to Germany before anyone else to negotiate with the Nazis about a possible cooperation. Like his patron and former boss in the "Jewish Department" in Berlin, Leopold von Mildenstein (cf. Krammer 2010, 112; Segev 1995, 45), Adolf Eichmann was invited by the Haganah in 1937 to travel to Palestine and visit the Zionist settlements in person. After a short stay in Haifa, Eichmann (accompanied by his travelling companion and superior Herbert Hagen from the „Jewish Department“<a href="#fn2" id="fn2back" class="backlink"><sup>ii</sup></a>) had to divert to Cairo under pressure from the British authorities.

The meeting there with Haganah agent Feivel Polkes was documented in detail and reflects the political priorities of Zionism in the information exchanged: on the one hand, the Hagana's approval of the policies in Nazi Germany was conveyed, as it would ensure a Jewish majority in Palestine. On the other hand, the location of a pirate radio station belonging to the Communist Party was revealed, and the Islamic religious community in Germany was accused of having links to Pan-Arabism and therefore indirectly to the Soviet Union (cf. Krammer 2010, 122). Anti-communist and Islamophobic – the Haganah's line at the time was not so different from today's Zionist positions. But even before this trip, there had been a meeting between the Nazis and the Hagana agent in Berlin. Let's read what Nicosia writes about this:
<i>"With Dr Franz Reichert from the German News Bureau (DNB), the SD also had an agent in Jerusalem. Reichert maintained contacts with Feivel Polkes, a member of the Zionist military organisation Haganah, who served as his source of information on Jewish and Arab activities. Reichert arranged for Polkes to visit Germany to meet with representatives of the Gestapo and SD. The meeting took place in Berlin from 26 February to 2 March 1937. Polkes, who was travelling through Europe and North America on an official mission to solicit financial support and weapons for the Haganah, explained to the German authorities his own and the Haganah's anti-English, anti-Arab and anti-Communist stance, as well as the desire to cooperate with Germany on the speedy and orderly emigration of German Jews to Palestine, which he saw as contributing to the establishment of an independent Jewish state." (Nicosia 1989, 387–388) </i>

In another book, he dealt with the same episode in even greater detail, mentioning that Polkes also "... <i>promised to represent German interests in the Middle East and even claimed that he could secure Germany access to oil and other resources in the region if Berlin did everything in its power to enable Jews to emigrate to Palestine." (Nicosia 2012, 169)</i>

This was not the only cooperation between the Nazis and Zionism, which was not an alternative to anti-Semitism, but only a particularly perfidious variant of it, as it saw itself primarily as a counter-movement to assimilation and the emancipation of Judaism. Above all, he shared the anti-Semites' view that Judaism was not a religion but a separate national ethnicity – in the Zionist parlance of the time, a "race"i<a href="#fn3" id="fn3back" class="backlink"><sup>ii</sup></a> – and that peaceful coexistence with non-Jews could never work. Because of these ideological similarities, Eichmann described himself as a Zionist who, according to his own statement, had only read two significant books in his life which had been recommended to him by his predecessor in the so-called Jewish Department, Baron von Mildenstein, namely Herzl's The Jewish State and Adolf Böhm's discussion of "the Zionist movement". Like him, at least in the early years of their rule, many Nazis regarded Zionism as a useful tool for their anti-Semitic policies and a guarantee for the smooth transfer of the Jewish population from Germany.

<h2 class="subtitle">The transformation from wanted terrorists to the Israeli army</h2>

Among the most notorious attacks carried out by Zionist terrorist organisations during the British Mandate were the bombing of the King David Hotel by the Irgun, which killed over 90 people, the assassination of UN envoy Folke Bernadotte, the attack on the Sinai railway, which killed 63 people, and many more. Not only did they accept casualties among the Palestinian civilian population in pursuit of their goals, but their actions were also directed against politicians and citizens of allied countries, other Zionist factions, or any civilians who happened to be present. There were also hundreds of Jewish victims when bombs were thrown at markets or cafés (Segev 1995, 57), wells were contaminated (cf. Pappé 2007, 164), peaceful villages were attacked, or refugee ships were sunk.

Even before the withdrawal of the British army, Zionist terrorist groups formed the actual military front to drive out the local population and realise the Zionist plans for the ethnic cleansing of Palestine. It was not only the Irgun or Lehi, but also the Haganah and its elite unit Palmach that committed horrific war crimes during the Nakba, such as on 1 May 1948 in the Palestinian village of Ayn al-Zaytun near Safad, one month after the well-known massacre of Deir Yasin (cf. Pappé 2007, 179–182). The brutality used was not only due to the sadism of the respective terrorist militias, but was systematically employed and served primarily as a deterrent to induce the local population to flee.

However, in addition to cooperation, there were also conflicts and fighting between the various Zionist factions, which were also carried out by armed force.<a href="#fn4" id="fn4back" class="backlink"><sup>iv</sup></a> Sometimes the Haganah cooperated more closely with the British and supported them in arresting members of the more right-wing groups. At other times, however, it also allied itself with the other terrorist gangs against the British Mandate power (which corresponds to the opportunistic character of Zionism, which always allies itself with those forces that seem useful to it at the moment, but also abandons or even attacks these allies when it is advantageous to do so).

The numerous massacres during the Nakba were already coordinated between the various factions, which made it clear that their commonalities were ultimately stronger than any possible contradictions.

After the defeat of German fascism, Zionism's primary concern was to sweep its own inaction and even collaboration during the Nazi crimes under the carpet, to instrumentalise the Holocaust for its own interests as a self-appointed representative of victims, and to present itself as the future saviour from anti-Semitism. While Zionist terrorist organisations continued their attacks in Palestine and the Arab region, Mossad Aliah Bet intensified its efforts to facilitate illegal entry into Palestine. The Mossad Aliah Bet had been founded as a branch of the Haganah as early as 1939, but it was not until after the Second World War that it came to public attention, as the organisation stepped up its efforts to circumvent the British naval blockade at the time by smuggling immigrants from European countries and weapons into the Mandate territory.

After the state was founded, the terrorist organisations therefore allied themselves to form the Israeli army, despite all their differences and historical baggage. The IDF was formed from terrorists and underground militias. Membership in a Zionist terrorist organisation was by no means an obstacle, but rather a prerequisite for a political career in the newly founded Israel. Well-known politicians such as Begin, Shamir, but also Rabin began as terrorists and ended up on the diplomatic stage as celebrated state terrorists or even Nobel Peace Prize winners. The Irgun became the centre of a new party, first called Cherut and later Likud. This party provides a direct link between former terrorists and later prime ministers such as Shamir and Begin, and Benyamin Netanyahu, who is currently wanted for war crimes.

<h2 class="subtitle">The Nakam episode</h2>

In 1945, shortly before the end of the war, a new terrorist organisation was founded, the only one that sought to cultivate an anti-fascist image. Its name was Nakam ("revenge") and, in keeping with this name, its official aim was to take revenge on the Germans for the Holocaust – in accordance with the theory of collective guilt, this applied to all Germans, regardless of whether they were Nazis or anti-fascists, children or soldiers, Allies or Holocaust survivors. Nakam therefore planned to poison the drinking water in Nuremberg and other cities such as Hamburg, Frankfurt and Munich with arsenic, which Chaim Weizmann had procured for them in Palestine (cf. Tobias and Zinke 2000, 32). Their slogan was: "Six million Germans for six million Jews". The Haganah supported them in this, but there are also reports claiming that some of their fighters probably betrayed and sabotaged the operation (cf. Segev 1995, 192–208). Ultimately, the "anti-German" stance in Israel was to be quickly abandoned and reparations were to be negotiated (which were paid not to the actual victims, but to the future Israeli state). A successful poison attack on millions of Germans would certainly not have been conducive to this.

When this operation failed because Nakam founder Abba Kovner was arrested and the arsenic hidden in milk cans was dumped into the sea, another plan was put into action: bread was poisoned in a Nuremberg internment camp for imprisoned Germans (including SS officers). Many inmates fell ill, but information about deceased prisoners remains contradictory to this day. According to various reports, up to 400 people are believed to have died, while others claim that all patients survived the attack (Segev 1995, 192–208). In any case, an American newspaper wrote of over 2,000 people who fell ill and had to be treated in hospitals.

Other activities of Nakam were questionable acts of revenge, which led even allied Zionists to distance themselves, such as the arbitrary killing of German citizens and the systematic rape of German women (cf. Segev 1995, 203). Meir Jari of Hashomer accused Kovner of advocating "para-fascist concepts" and wanted to send him and his accomplices for "re-education" (Segev 1995, 196).

After the Nakba, typical terrorist methods were continued by the Israeli state, which had been proclaimed in 1948, its army and its secret services, for example targeted killings, letter bombs, detonated infrastructure, cyber attacks and even exploding pagers and mobile phones. These actions differed from the terrorist attacks of the Zionist underground only in their increased quantity. Today, the Israeli secret services are taking over the tasks that were previously carried out by the wanted Zionist terrorist organisations. The fact that the Western public perception, according to which Israel must protect itself from terrorism and is in no way based on terrorism itself, is completely different is primarily due to media manipulation and propaganda.

The background to this false flag operation was Israel's intention to encourage Jews from Arab countries to flee to Palestine and to disrupt the centuries-long peaceful coexistence between Jewish and non-Jewish Iraqis. The attack was one of several Mossad assassinations in Iraq and other Arab countries with devastating consequences for the Jewish population.  Avi Shlaim recounts this episode in his book <i>3 Worlds: Memoirs of an Arab-Jew</i> (Shlaim 2024). Erich Fried, in turn, wrote in his poem "The Hand" about a Jewish plaintiff who demanded compensation from the Israeli state for his hand, which he had lost in this bombing of an Iraqi synagogue (Fried 2021).

<h2 class="subtitle">Conclusion </h2>

If terrorism and resistance are to be more than interchangeable terms and arbitrary attributions:

While war is known to be terrorism with a higher budget, today both resistance legitimised under international law and any opposition to armament and war are accused of being terrorist. The dominant power of definition, rather than the severity or consequences of an act, decides whether the act is labelled terrorism, self-defence or resistance.

In this context, it is striking how much reverence the Zionist terrorist groups Irgun, Stern and Hagana/Palmach still enjoy today from official Israel, with museums, stamps, statues and other artefacts dedicated to honouring the terrorists. Even if surviving underground fighters speak about the atrocities they committed, there are no negative consequences for them. On the contrary, many former terrorists have had successful careers in the Zionist state, because they switched from terrorism to state terrorism and were proud of their actions until their deaths. They considered themselves founding fathers and pioneers, without whose criminal activities the modern state of Israel could not have been established.

Outside Israel and its romanticised view of the "underground fighters," however, the IDF's roots in terrorism are a chapter of Zionist history that is mostly kept hidden. The continuity from this starting point to the current genocide in Gaza, which is absurdly sold as an "anti-terrorist operation" in the Western media and by political accomplices, has not yet been sufficiently researched scientifically, but is still considered taboo. It is high time to close this research gap, which would not only broaden the theoretical horizon, but should also have very practical consequences for relations with Israel.
