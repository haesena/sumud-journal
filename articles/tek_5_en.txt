<h2 class="subtitle">Introduction</h2>

Postcolonial studies is an interdisciplinary, poststructuralist, and critical discipline. In this context, many postcolonial theorists view the colonization of Palestine and the establishment of Israel as a continuation of a form of colonialism that was long believed to have been overcome.  They therefore advocate for Palestine to be decolonized and granted the same freedoms and rights as all other sovereign states and peoples. This discourse—as articulated in particular by various proponents of postcolonial theory—was suddenly called into question after October 7, 2023.

Especially in German-speaking countries, the controversial topic of Palestine was fiercely debated in Western academic circles. In particular, scholars in postcolonial studies argued that Israel is a settler-colonial, racist apartheid regime, while opponents generally discredited this stance in the mainstream Western media as anti-Semitic (Beyer 2024; Klein 2024; Neff 2023; Schröter 2023).

Have these debates in the West shown that there is no longer any tolerance for differing opinions? For in these debates, critics of postcolonialism in particular have argued that criticism of Israel can, in principle, only be motivated by anti-Semitism. This position assumes that Israel must not be criticized at all and constitutes an obstacle both to freedom of expression and thought and to a possible resolution of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict.

At the same time, in this discourse, supporters of postcolonial studies—who have traditionally leaned to the left—are accused of collaborating with Islamists and showing solidarity with Hamas. Experts familiar with postcolonial studies, however, argue that certain points are being deliberately misunderstood in order to prevent necessary debates from taking place.

Why are postcolonial theory and its proponents barred from criticizing Israel? Isn’t it the very purpose of postcolonial discourse to engage with the so-called Middle East conflict just as it does with all other conflicts and wars in which Western colonialism has been involved? And what do these restrictions on free thought mean for the future of postcolonial studies? I would like to attempt to answer these questions against the backdrop of current debates.

<h2 class="subtitle">Why are postcolonial studies and their proponents barred from criticizing Israel?</h2>

Postcolonial studies and decolonial discourse are accused — especially in leading German-language media outlets, newspapers, and magazines — of being too critical and anti-Semitic. This criticism is directed against a stance that opposes colonialism and imperialism. When Judith Butler received the Adorno Prize in 2012, she was told that her postcolonial feminist perspective was “anti-Semitic,” and in 2019, the well-known Cameroonian philosopher Achille Mbembe was accused of anti-Semitism. Unsurprisingly, this criticism was leveled after Butler and Mbembe had criticized Israel. Furthermore, there have been and continue to be attempts to denigrate postcolonial studies as a whole, a trend reflected in the debates about German colonialism that arise from time to time.

Since October 7, 2023, criticism and attacks against postcolonial studies have been on the rise.  This criticism is particularly prevalent in the German-language media. Many examples can be found, such as in <i>Der Standard</i> (Schröter 2023) in Austria, in the <i>NZZ</i> (Neff 2023) in Switzerland, or in <i>Der Spiegel</i> (Beyer 2024) and the <i>FAZ</i> (Klein 2024) in Germany. A closer look at these texts reveals that they constitute a systematic attack on postcolonial studies en (Hausbichler 2025).

<i>“Postcolonialism has become a new bogeyman. For weeks, article after article has appeared in German-language and Anglo-Saxon cultural sections that view ‘postcolonialism’ as the cause of many problems—above all, it is said to be responsible for inadequate responses from politics and academia to the massacres of October 7 in Israel.”</i> (Purtschert, Lüthi, and Falk 2024)

Particularly in the writings of those intellectuals who are determined to defend Israel at all costs, the stance of postcolonial studies against colonialism and imperialism was the focus of criticism. In this criticism, the accusation of antisemitism was used to generally discredit postcolonial studies.

The phenomenon of equating anti-Zionism with anti-Semitism affected not only representatives of post al studies but also, for example, anti-Zionist or Israel-critical Jews. In this context, many Jewish intellectuals and activists were confronted with this accusation. For this reason, many postcolonial scholars and Jewish intellectuals have lost their jobs or funding for their programs and projects (Rau 2024).

In an interview with the Austrian newspaper Der Standard, Nikita Dhawan, a leading figure in postcolonial theory, criticized this situation as follows:

“I am particularly skeptical when Jewish intellectuals are accused of antisemitism. This is happening constantly in Germany and Austria right now—just think of Omri Böhm at the Vienna Festival last year. It affects Susan Neiman, Masha Gessen, Judith Butler, Eyal Weizman, and Omri Böhm, among others. The list goes on” (Hausbichler 2025).
Postcolonial studies are directed against colonialism and racism, which is why these false accusations of antisemitism affect them particularly (since antisemitism is, after all, a specific form of racism). These accusations are directed not only against postcolonial theorists and activists, but over time also against socialists, liberals, and, as already mentioned, Jewish people who criticize Israel (cf. Sarbo 2024).

Vivek Chibber, an American Marxist sociologist known for his critique of postcolonial studies, raises important points of criticism in this field with his work « Postcolonial Theory and the Specter of Capital » (2013, German ed. 2018). Chibber emphasizes, however, that it is pointless to accuse postcolonial studies of antisemitism: <i>“That is the simple and broad definition of postcolonial theory. But whatever it may be—there is no trace of systematic antisemitism in it. I think this accusation can only be made by someone who equates criticism of Israel with anti-Semitism, which is absurd in every conceivable way”</i> (Sarbo 2024).

Dhawan, a postcolonial theorist already cited, goes even further and describes the situation as follows: “Hannah Arendt would not have received the Hannah Arendt Prize today. Because of her political stance on Israel and her views on Zionism, she would likely have been ‘canceled’ in Germany and Austria as well. I hope the irony is not lost on anyone” (Hausbichler 2025).

It is well known that criticism of post-colonial studies and of activist discourses, leveled under the banner of antisemitism, does not actually present any scholarly — or even moral — arguments. It should therefore be acknowledged that these accusations in fact undermine the values that have been taught in the West for decades. An academic discipline should not be simply and rudely excluded merely because it has voiced unwelcome criticism, especially since postcolonial criticism of Israel is directed against colonialism in general.

This critique is specifically based on three concepts: settler colonialism, the apartheid system, and decolonization. Let us now examine how these concepts are specifically applied to Israel.

<h2 class="subtitle">Settler colonialism</h2>

The biggest problem in Palestine is colonization. Under settler colonialism,,  Palestinians are expelled from their land over a specific period of timeand new Israeli settlers are forcibly settled in their place at the behest of the Israeli state. In this way, Israel removes Palestinians from the territory and replaces them with Israeli citizens, causing the indigenous population to lose all its rights and culture while a new power is established. This is a key difference between settler colonialism and classical colonialism. Author Pappé emphasizes that the term “settler colonialism” is more applicable to Israel than the general concept of colonialism, which is mostly applied to historical events ( Pappé 2017, 178).

I would distinguish between colonialism and settler colonialism primarily on one point. In classical colonialism, a powerful country invades a weaker one and uses its military to occupy a country or region for a certain period of time in order to exploit it. The colonial army then withdraws, however, and every soldier is glad to leave the country and return to their homeland. But in settler colonialism, the settlers remain until the land is finally completely assimilated or the indigenous population is driven out.

The goal of settler colonialism is to impose its culture — which it considers superior —through oppression, while simultaneously erasing all cultural traces of the indigenous population. In many cities and villages, the social and cultural heritage is obscured by the assignment of new names. In Palestine, immigrants resettled by Israel have the right to use heavy weapons in this area. In this way, the new settlers can more easily harass their Palestinian neighbors, confiscate their homes, land, and resources, and, after a certain period of time, hasten their complete withdrawal from the area. By offering incentives to the settlers, the Israeli state makes it clear to them that these areas definitely belong to them.

Marxist sociologist Vivek Chibber emphasizes that Israel occupies Palestine and that this form of land seizure can not be described as anyhing other than settler colonialism:

“Since 1967, Israel has quite obviously acted as a colonial power toward Gaza and the West Bank. It has illegally occupied them. The citizens in these territories do not have the same rights and are subjected to a slow process of ethnic cleansing through settlement expansion. I don’t know what to call this if not settler colonialism” (Sarbo 2024).
This situation has inevitably led to Israel being referred to in the literature as a settler-colonizer. This policy is the actual proof that Israel systematically exploits the Palestinians. In other words, Israel is stripping Palestinian land of its sovereignty by seeking to transfer the population. It is only natural that this situation is criticized by postcolonial scholarship, even if Israel’s land grab does not constitute classical colonialism, but rather settler colonialism. Historian Ilan Pappé argues that Zionism in Israel behaves like other settler colonies and that the colonization of Palestine is similar to European colonization of South and North America, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand ( Pappé 2017, 59).

<h2 class="subtitle">The Apartheid System</h2>

One of the most important characteristics of settler colonialism is the apartheid system. In this context, Israel has established an apartheid regime through the systematic and institutionalized discrimination against Palestinians, both those living in Israel and those living in the occupied Palestinian territories. Under the apartheid regime established by Israel against the Palestinians, Palestinians are systematically oppressed, dominated, controlled, and discriminated against through laws and practices. The apartheid system has existed since May 1948 and is growing every day. To name just a few examples: forced migration, confiscation of property, administrative detention, torture, unlawful injury and killing of people, deprivation of fundamental civil liberties, and different laws for Israeli Jews and Palestinians are applied systematically and institutionally. Through the establishment of the apartheid system, Israel systematically treats Palestinians unequally.

The apartheid practices implemented by Israel in the Palestinian territories are perhaps among the worst when compared to other countries . The Cameroonian philosopher Achille Mbembe emphasized that the apartheid regime in Palestine is even more deadly than in South Africa: <i>“It is not apartheid, South African style. It is far more lethal.”</i> (Mbembe 2015, vii)

Western states, in particular, are reluctant to condemn Israel’s apartheid practices, and Israel, bolstered by this stance, continues its practices. Apartheid involves violations of public law and crimes against humanity. In this context, according to many human rights organizations and independent academic reports, Israel has pursued discriminatory policies against Palestinians.

This is also evident from Amnesty International’s report “Israel’s Apartheid Against Palestinians” dated February 1, 2022. In addition to Amnesty, there are many reports, documentaries<a href="#fn1" id="fn1back" class="backlink"><sup>i</sup></a>, and books on this apartheid regime. B’Tselem also documents such practices (B’Tselem 2024). This systematic oppression falls under the category of crimes against humanity <i>“within the meaning of the Rome Statute” and constitutes “denial of basic human rights” that “prevent the racial group or groups from participating in the political, social, economic, and cultural life of the country, as well as the deliberate creation of conditions preventing [its or their] full development” under the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination."</i> (Amnesty International 2022, 263)

<h2 class="subtitle">Decolonization</h2>

With the establishment of Israel, the Palestinian people were divided in their own land and subsequently placed under occupation through Israel’s systematic settler-colonial policies and the apartheid system it has established, while more and more of their land is being taken from them day by day. The process of decolonization begins and ends with the decision to free oneself from this occupation and exploitation. The history of the Palestinian people has shown that this resistance will continue until the Zionist occupiers withdraw from the occupied Palestinian territories.
For those who view postcolonial studies as a disciplinary method, it is nearly impossible not to use these three concepts to criticize Israel in light of the events in Palestine. This is because the arguments presented are based on realistic and scientific findings.

For those who share a Eurocentric perspective, the arguments mentioned above are invalid and do not apply to Israel. Furthermore, in their view, anyone who puts forward these arguments is automatically an anti-Semite. All counterarguments are generally interpreted in this way, but they have no scientific validity.

<h2 class="subtitle">Isn’t the very purpose of postcolonial discourse to address the so-called Middle East conflict just as it does all other conflicts and wars in which Western colonialism was involved?</h2>

I would like to attempt to answer this question against the backdrop of current debates. Because <i>“post”</i> implies that something from the past is being analyzed, postcolonial studies are often advised not to address contemporary settler colonialism. However, this is a misunderstanding of Postk olonial S tudies , which also provide insights into current events. For as the occupation of Palestine demonstrates, colonialism exhibits continuity, which is why the problem of decolonization has not yet been resolved worldwide.

Israel was founded with the support of major colonial powers (such as Great Britain and the United States) at a time when classical colonialism was coming to an end and decolonization was beginning. As a product of its time, with an ideological background rooted in Western-origin Zionism and with the help of the Western countries that supported it, it nevertheless succeeded in carving out a space for itself by force in the region, which was a former British colony. On the other hand, Israel was influenced by and collaborated with the apartheid regime in South Africa, a discriminatory regime established during the same period. In this context, Israel viewed South Africa’s development as a model for its own future. As is well known, Western countries are the biggest supporters of the apartheid regime established by Israel. In this context, while Israel pursues a system that does not constitute classical colonialism, it receives all kinds of support from those who practiced this classical colonialism or whose settler colonialism (as in the U.S. or Australia) has already been completed or has entered a new phase. 

Some claim that the conflicts in the Middle East, particularly the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, are not directly related to colonialism, while others view it as an ethnic and religious conflict. Viewed from an academic perspective, an apartheid regime was established, and through the policy of settler colonialism, an attempt was made to completely drive the Palestinians out of the region by expanding its territory. Therefore, this is a form of settler colonialism that may differ from other forms of classical colonialism but certainly falls within the scope of postcolonial studies.

<h2 class="subtitle">What do these intellectual taboos mean for the future of postcolonial studies? <br />A Scapegoat Discipline: Postcolonial Studies</h2>

Since postcolonial studies seek to give voice to those labeled as “the Others,” they are more than just another academic discipline; consequently, due to their universalist approach, they are often discredited and regarded as taboo, particularly by pro-Israeli authors. Postcolonial theory draws on non-Western media and sources of information while consistently maintaining a critical stance. Postcolonialism views world history and culture not only from a Western perspective but also from the perspective of global cultures and ideas (Tek 2024, 16).

The main reason for the accusation of anti-Semitism—for which there is no scholarly argument—is the fact that thinking that deviates from the dominant narrative is increasingly rejected and, in some cases, even criminalized. It is becoming clear that there is no longer any scholarly justification for attempting to silence anyone who does not think about an issue in the same way as the so-called mainstream by questioning the one-sided policy toward Israel as advocated by Western governments with the support of the media. Those who continue to take this stance are opposing both their own history and fundamental scholarly principles.

A postcolonial perspective critical of Zionism was initially interpreted as anti-Semitism; later, some commentators emphasized that those who criticized Israel had misunderstood postcolonialism, since, as the name suggests, it should primarily concern itself with the past. Others claimed that postcolonial studies condoned violence and should therefore be banned. It is obvious that these arguments are solely intended to silence legitimate criticism of Israel, which in reality amounts to a ban on free thought.

The accusation of anti-Semitism leveled against postcolonial studies does indeed have an ideological, right-wing background. As well-known Swiss postcolonial researchers state: <i>“Postcolonial research is criticized as being too simplistic, too ‘woke,’ and anti-Semitic to boot. Yet these attacks are disingenuous and stem from right-wing conservative activism …. ”</i> (Purtschert, Lüthi, and Falk 2024).

This reaction is actually not surprising, since it runs counter to academic freedom to confront an entire branch of scholarship with such sweeping accusations. Aram Ziai (2024) of the University of Kassel comments:

<i>“If postcolonial studies demonstrate such sensitivity to contradictions and ambivalences, how do these unjustified accusations arise? In my view, several factors play a role here. For one thing, postcolonial studies have long been a thorn in the side of many right-wingers and conservatives due to their criticism of colonialism, imperialism, and Western superiority. Years ago, the AfD accused them of ‘intellectual terrorism’ and called for their abolition. In this context, the accusation of anti-Semitism is a welcome pretext: the first resolution against the campaign critical of Israel, ‘Boycott, Divestment, and Sanctions’ (BDS), also came from the AfD.”</i>&nbsp

The AfD parliamentary group has submitted a motion to the German Bundestag calling for an end to funding for postcolonial studies and for these funds to be redirected toward research into the connection between antisemitism and postcolonialism (AfD Parliamentary Group 2024). As can be seen, the accusations leveled against post al studies actually reuse arguments previously employed by the right-wing conservative movement. Attempts were made to ban this discipline, after which it was defamed by leveling accusations of antisemitism. Postcolonialism must remain critical precisely in order to challenge this system of Eurocentric arrogance and expose its right-wing conservative to fascist roots.

This senseless “strategy of accusing others of anti-Semitism” against post colonial studies also extends to other counter-movements: <i>“Climate movements and queer or feminist alliances have also become more open to anti-Semitic motives in recent years”</i> (Decker et al. 2024, 141). If we look at history, it would not be the first time that this strategy of fabricated accusations ultimately leads to the banning of dissenting views and the spread of a fascist-like ideology.

Chibber raises an important point when he notes that the blanket use of the term “anti-Semitism,” as employed in particular by defenders of Israel, has caused its original meaning to be lost: <i>“The accusation of anti-Semitism is the only arrow in the quiver of Israel’s defenders—and by using it so inflationarily, they undermine the historical significance of anti-Semitism as a real threat.”</i> (Sarbo 2024)

In fact, the concept of antisemitism is heading toward such an end, especially since it has been used as a weapon against anyone who still dares to criticize Israel after October 7, 2023.

This criticism of postcolonialism in the German-speaking public sphere actually shows that only conformist thinking is permitted anymore and that, due to these “difficult times,” voices opposing Eurocentrism are no longer granted freedom of thought. The very lack of a broader spectrum of opinion once again highlights the continued importance of postcolonial studies, which is why they should be promoted even more vigorously and consistently as a critical theory and discipline. For this reason, any society that still values critical thinking should continue to champion the discipline of postcolonial studies in the future.
