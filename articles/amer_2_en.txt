<h2 class="subtitle">1. Introduction</h2>

The unfolding catastrophe in Gaza demands an analytical frame that exceeds the limits of conventional humanitarian discourse. While humanitarian frameworks are indispensable for cataloguing displacement and infrastructural collapse, they often remain related to a biologized vocabulary of life and need that can depoliticize violence and obscure its structural design (Reid 2010). What is occurring in Gaza is not merely a humanitarian emergency; it is the systematic dismantling of a social, intellectual, and cultural ecosystem — a project of erasure that is as epistemic and civilizational as it is material (Amer 2026, forthcoming). This article advances scholasticide as a concept for apprehending the intentional destruction of knowledge systems, educational institutions, and future-making capacities. Rather than an incidental byproduct of urban warfare, scholasticide is an epistemic strategy. It is what de Sousa Santos (2015) describes as epistemicide, the murder of knowledge—through which a people’s ability to produce, transmit, and preserve its history is extinguished. 

Reports from international bodies have described the wholesale destruction of Gaza’s education system as a deliberate means of depriving an entire population of its intellectual leadership (United Nations 2024). Archives, libraries, and universities become strategic nodes in an architecture of domination that targets the conditions of knowledge itself. The report highlights the ongoing and extensive destruction in Gaza, where Israeli military actions have led to significant loss of life and damage to infrastructure. It frames this destruction as part of a systematic campaign against the Palestinian people, describing acts that could be interpreted as genocidal. The Special Rapporteur argues that the violence is not random but part of a long-term strategy aimed at displacing Palestinians and erasing their presence in the occupied territories. This includes targeting essential services, leading to humanitarian crises and further displacement. The report calls for urgent intervention from member states to prevent further atrocities and suggests that the current situation reflects a failure of international law to protect Palestinian rights. It emphasizes the need for accountability and the application of the Genocide Convention.

Education is never neutral terrain. As Foucault (1972) argues, knowledge production is a primary site of power and resistance. The assault on Gaza’s higher education infrastructure—reportedly encompassing the destruction of its twelve higher education institutions—constitutes an attack on the capacity to imagine, narrate, and construct collective futures. Complementary assessments indicate that the destruction or damage of the vast majority of school buildings has opened a generational gap undermining social reproduction and foreclosing the very preconditions for recovery. In this sense, scholasticide is inseparable from broader strategies that render populations governable through the management of precarity and the erosion of intellectual sovereignty. UNICEF (2025) reports that more than 97% of schools have been damaged or destroyed, and 91.8% of all educational facilities require full reconstruction or major rehabilitation. 

This paper argues such a big percentage would definitely lead to a <i>lost generation</i>, and emphasizes that children have experienced three consecutive years without school and that education collapse risks producing an entire generation with no access to learning. In this context, this article is written as scholarship and as testimony. Writing from the position of a displaced academic and survivor, it adopts a reflexive mode that integrates conceptual analysis with lived experience. Displacement here is not a singular event but a continuous condition of emotional and intellectual erosion—a form of psychocide—in which one attempts to teach, research, and write under the perpetual threat of familial annihilation, hunger, and bombardment.

As  Fassin (2011) argues, humanitarian reason foregrounds immediate relief and the ethics of compassion in ways that can displace political causality and structural analysis, thereby obscuring the deeper ruptures that violence produces. In Gaza, academic life has been displaced into tents, shelters, and overcrowded refuges; classrooms have become rubble, shelters, or mass graves; intellectual continuity is broken even as survival demands persist.

Framing Gaza primarily as a humanitarian crisis risks masking the structural logic of the violence. This paper argues that scholasticide is a form of structural violence operating across humanitarian, political, epistemic, and civilizational dimensions. By destroying the pipeline for future doctors, engineers, teachers, and scholars, scholasticide ensures a durable fragility. It is therefore a war not only on bodies, but on knowledge; not only on people, but on the conditions of future existence. The aim is not simply to interrupt learning in the present, but to sever the continuity between a people’s past and their possible futures.

Accordingly, the paper conceptualizes scholasticide as the war on knowledge beyond humanitarian crisis, reframing education not as a secondary casualty of conflict but as a central site of violence. It contributes to emerging scholarship on epistemicide, culturicide, and structural violence by grounding these frameworks in the lived realities of Gaza’s academic and student communities. Methodologically, this is a conceptual–theoretical analysis that develops a critical framework for understanding scholasticide as structural violence and generational destruction. Rather than empirical data analysis, it employs conceptual synthesis, theoretical integration, and reflexive scholarship grounded in lived experience to outline a de scholasticide framework for education protection, continuity, and reconstruction.

<h2 class="subtitle">2. Conceptual Framework: Defining Scholasticide</h2>

This paper conceptualizes scholasticide as a structural process of knowledge destruction in which educational systems, intellectual communities, and generational continuity are systematically dismantled through physical, bureaucratic, economic, and political violence. Far from accidental or episodic, scholasticide is cumulative and processual—an architecture of erasure that produces long term societal collapse and what may be described as future theft. It is not simply the interruption of education, but the dismantling of the infrastructures and relationships that make knowledge production, transmission, and preservation possible. Scholasticide is distinct from incidental damage to education during conflict. It refers instead to a strategy of domination that targets the conditions of intellectual sovereignty, transforming education from a site of development into a terrain of vulnerability and extinction. At its core, scholasticide is the collapse of generational continuity (Amer 2026, forthcoming).

<h3 class="subsubtitle">2.1 Dimensions of Scholasticide</h3>

While the physical destruction of buildings is its most visible manifestation, scholasticide encompasses a much wider field of harm. Following de Sousa Santos (2015), whose work on epistemicide highlights the systematic silencing of subordinated knowledge systems, this framework identifies five interlocking dimensions:

<ul class="dashlist">

	<li>Infrastructural: The physical destruction of schools, universities, libraries, laboratories, archives, and research centers.</li>
	
	<li>Human: The killing, displacement, intimidation, and suppression of educators, scholars, students, administrators, and intellectual workers.</li>
	
	<li>Institutional: The collapse of governance systems, accreditation bodies, academic autonomy, and mechanisms of quality assurance.</li>
	
	<li>Epistemic: The erasure of linguistic, cultural, and historical knowledge systems; the destruction of archives and repositories of memory.</li>
	
	<li>Temporal: The rupture of intergenerational continuity, severing the pedagogical, professional, and cultural pipelines through which societies reproduce knowledge and future capacity.</li>

</ul>

Through these dimensions, scholasticide dissolves both the material foundations and the social relations necessary for intellectual life.

<h3 class="subsubtitle">2.2 Scholasticide as Structural and Slow Violence</h3>

Framing scholasticide as structural violence shifts the analysis away from narratives of collateral damage and toward the deeper architecture of harm.  As Galtung (1969)  argues, structural violence is embedded in political and economic systems that constrain the realization of human potential. In Gaza, this form of violence is acutely visible in the collapse of the education sector. With more than 97% of schools damaged or destroyed and many converted into overcrowded shelters, education has ceased to function as a stabilizing social institution, undermining the infrastructures of social reproduction, civic participation, and cultural continuity (UNICEF 2025). In this framework, knowledge itself constitutes protected civilian capital. Universities, libraries, and archives are not neutral spaces; they are repositories of collective memory and identity. Their destruction constitutes an assault on civilian life at its deepest structural level.

Rodgers' and O’Neill's (2012) theorization of infrastructural violence reveals how the destruction of the systems that enable ordinary life—schools, water, shelter, mobility—produces forms of harm that exceed discrete acts of force. Nixon's (2011) account of slow violence similarly captures the long arc of destruction that accumulates through blockade, economic attrition, and bureaucratic restriction. In Gaza, this long-term erosion has culminated in a catastrophic collapse: over 97% of schools damaged or destroyed and numerous others converted into overcrowded shelters, leaving education structurally incapacitated even before bombardment intensified.

Within this framework, scholasticide is not the destruction of education within conflict; it is the destruction of education as a mode of conflict. It is the collapse of generational continuity.

<h3 class="subsubtitle">2.3 The Processual Nature of Erasure</h3>

Scholasticide in Gaza must be understood as a long-term strategy rather than a recent or isolated phenomenon. Its roots stretch back decades, intensifying over the past 17 years of blockade and recurring military assaults. The blockade has created conditions of intellectual imprisonment, systematically restricting the mobility of students and faculty, freezing infrastructure development, and severing universities from global academic networks. Essential academic practices—conference participation, research collaboration, laboratory procurement—have been rendered nearly impossible. This isolation has been compounded by repeated cycles of physical destruction. The Islamic University of Gaza was heavily damaged during military operations in 2008–2009 and again in 2014, with major university facilities repeatedly targeted and destroyed long before the current escalation (Middle East Eye 2024).

The cumulative impact of these assaults — widespread damage to laboratories, classrooms, and research facilities — produced long term material and institutional losses that weakened Gaza’s higher education sector even before the present catastrophe. Yet the architecture of scholasticide also operates through bureaucratic control. Permit regimes, checkpoints, and mobility restrictions turn education into administratively governed terrain. Academic autonomy erodes as routine functions — hiring, promotions, student admissions, procurement — become vulnerable to external interdiction.

Economic strangulation intensifies this fragility. Universities reliant on tuition in an economy marked by mass unemployment face chronic fiscal instability, reduced staffing, and infrastructure decay. This structural precarity makes institutions acutely vulnerable to collapse when subjected to additional shocks.

Taken together, these mechanisms demonstrate that scholasticide is not a singular act of destruction but a layered, cumulative process that erodes intellectual systems over time.

<h2 class="subtitle">3. Gaza as a Case Study: The Mechanics of Scholasticide</h2>

Gaza represents one of the clearest contemporary manifestations of scholasticide as a structural, cumulative process of knowledge destruction. Here, the dismantling of the education system operates through multi-layered, interlocking forms of violence that target institutions, people, infrastructures, and generational continuity simultaneously. The transformation of universities from vibrant knowledge hubs into shelters, ruins, or militarized zones marks a profound rupture in the social and epistemic fabric of Palestinian life. This case illustrates how scholasticide transforms education from a vehicle of social mobility into a terrain of mere survival, producing leadership vacuums, governance fragility, and long-term intellectual dependency (de Sousa Santos 2015; Nixon 2011). In Gaza, scholasticide is neither incidental nor episodic; it is systemic, embedded in decades of blockade, infrastructural attrition, and recurring cycles of destruction.

<h3 class="subsubtitle">3.1 Life Under Siege: Education as Sumūd (Steadfastness)</h3>

Prior to October 2023, Gaza was a living society under siege, where education served as a central pillar of resilience. Despite a seventeen year blockade, Palestinian communities preserved one of the strongest educational profiles in the region, with near universal primary enrollment and some of the lowest illiteracy rates globally (Faculty of Education & UNRWA 2024) In this context, education functioned as a form of <i>sumūd</i>—steadfastness—and as a practice of “everyday resistance” essential to dignity-preservation under settler colonial constraint (Shalhoub-Kevorkian 2015). 

Higher education in Gaza has long transcended the boundaries of formal instruction. Universities have operated as repositories of cultural identity, historical consciousness, and moral infrastructure—spaces where studying constitutes an assertion of existence and teaching becomes a refusal of erasure. The targeting of these institutions therefore strikes at the foundational structures through which Palestinian society maintains continuity, cohesion, and future oriented aspiration (Shalhoub-Kevorkian 2015).

<h3 class="subsubtitle">3.2 Collapse into Catastrophe: The Necropolitics of Endurance </h3>

The escalation beginning in October 2023 marked a radical rupture in Gaza’s social world. Continuous bombardment, mass displacement, and infrastructural collapse transformed the region from a besieged society into a condition of precarious life, where survival displaces social reproduction as the organizing logic of existence (Butler 2009). Education became structurally impossible: classrooms ceased to function, schools were destroyed or repurposed as shelters, and learning was replaced by the urgent logics of securing food, water, and safety (United Nations News 2025). In these conditions, Gaza resembles a necropolitical order in which the management of life and death reorganizes social space around endurance (Mbembe 2003). In such an environment, intellectual life is not merely disrupted; it is erased from the rhythms of daily existence, making the collapse of education both a symptom of humanitarian catastrophe and a mechanism of deeper structural disintegration.

<h3 class="subsubtitle">3.3 Scholasticide as a Genocidal Process  </h3>

Scholasticide in Gaza functions simultaneously as a mechanism and evidence of genocidal processes. By targeting the foundations of knowledge, memory, and generational reproduction, it attacks the conditions under which collective existence is sustained. This aligns with E. L. Lemkin’s formulation of genocide as not only the physical destruction of a people but also the systematic dismantling of the institutional, cultural, and intellectual structures through which group life is reproduced (Lemkin 1944). Current documentation illustrates this dynamic with devastating clarity. The destruction of Gaza’s education system has produced what may be termed future erasure—the elimination of a society’s capacity to regenerate its professional, civic, and intellectual leadership. According to Euro Med Human Rights Monitor (2024), Israeli forces have killed 94 university professors, hundreds of teachers, and thousands of students, while every university in Gaza has been destroyed or rendered inoperative through bombardment or detonation. Complementary investigations report the widespread destruction of archives, libraries, and cultural repositories, the systematic bombing of university campuses, and the killing of academic leaders and intellectuals across disciplines. 

These actions collectively sever the pathways through which knowledge, identity, and social continuity are transmitted across generations. In this sense, scholasticide is not merely educational destruction; it is the destruction of collective futurity, the deliberate unmaking of a people’s capacity to imagine, rebuild, and exist beyond catastrophe.

<h3 class="subsubtitle">3.4 The Education–Health–Governance Nexus</h3>

The collapse of educational infrastructure in Gaza generates cascading failures across all civilian sectors, illustrating that scholasticide is not an isolated educational crisis but a systemic, society wide disintegration. 

<h4>Health System Collapse</h4>

Education is the foundation of medical training; without universities and medical schools, no future doctors, nurses, midwives, or allied health workers can be produced. In Gaza, the destruction of both medical schools—at the Islamic University of Gaza and Al Azhar University—has already halted the training pipeline for future health professionals. This has directly accelerated the collapse of an already overwhelmed health system. 

Shellah (2025) reports that “two medical schools located in the Gaza Strip … have been destroyed,” and that this destruction has resulted in hundreds of medical students losing clinical rotations, a situation which “contributed to a shortage of healthcare workers,” amplifying system collapse. Asmar (2025) confirms that as of mid 2024, only 17 of Gaza’s 36 hospitals remained partially operational, with 885 health workers killed, creating catastrophic gaps in care capacity. 

Barakat and Milton (2015)similarly argue that higher education is essential for post conflict recovery and for producing the human capital required to sustain healthcare, governance, and reconstruction. 

<h4>Governance and Institutional Capacity</h4>

Education is the generator of administrators, civil servants, planners, legal professionals, and public sector leadership. Without functioning universities, the state’s governance capacity collapses, even if physical infrastructure were to be rebuilt. Barakat and Milton (2015)  emphasize that higher education plays a strategic role in stabilizing war torn societies, rebuilding governance institutions, and preventing institutional failure. They describe higher education as integral to “restoring collapsed governance” and rebuilding state capacity. Thus, scholasticide produces long term administrative paralysis and makes any future governance fragile and dependent on external actors.

<h4>Reconstruction and Infrastructural Integrity</h4>

Engineers, architects, environmental scientists, and urban planners—essential for rebuilding homes, hospitals, roads, water networks, energy grids, and sanitation systems—are all produced by higher education institutions. The destruction of Gaza’s universities is total: every university has been destroyed or rendered inoperative (Euro Med Human Rights Monitor 2024), eliminating the professional base required for reconstruction. UN analyses show that “more than 97% of schools have been damaged or destroyed,” further erasing pathways to the technical and vocational training needed for rebuilding civilian infrastructure (UNICEF 2025).Without trained professionals, reconstruction becomes technically impossible and structurally unsustainable.

<h4>Social and Psychological Fragmentation</h4>

Educational collapse fractures social cohesion and deepens trauma, producing long term psychological and communal harm.

<ul class="dashlist">
	
	<li>UNICEF’s 2025 Situation Report notes that 450,000 children face “mass displacement trauma,” with over 70% experiencing severe psychological distress, exacerbated by the loss of routine, community, and learning environments.</li>
	
	<li>United Nations News (2025) documents that classrooms have become overcrowded shelters, erasing safe learning spaces and leaving children with “no education” and severe psychosocial strain.</li>
	
	<li>The generational rupture produced by interrupted schooling disrupts identity formation, cognitive development, and the social bonds that sustain community resilience.</li>

</ul>

<h4>Synthesis: Scholasticide as Societal Disintegration</h4>

Taken together, these failures reveal that scholasticide is not a sector-specific loss but a multi-system collapse:

<ul class="dashlist">
	
	<li>It eliminates the health workforce pipeline,</li>
	
	<li>destroys the governance and administrative base,</li>
	
	<li>prevents reconstruction by eliminating engineers and planners, and</li>
	
	<li>fractures social cohesion, producing cumulative trauma and long term instability.</li>

</ul>

These dynamics illustrate that scholasticide is a form of societal disintegration, targeting the entire ecology of civilian life and the very conditions through which societies sustain themselves over time.

<h4>Gaza in Historical Continuum: Scholasticide Before 2023</h4>

While the post 2023 destruction is unprecedented in scale, it is embedded in a longer trajectory of intellectual and infrastructural attrition. Since the imposition of the blockade in 2007, Gaza has experienced a chronic regime of restriction that systematically undermined higher education and knowledge infrastructures—what can be conceptualized as intellectual imprisonment. The key components of this system included:

Movement restrictions that curtailed academic mobility, faculty exchanges, and student travel for conferences, scholarships, and clinical rotations, progressively isolating Gaza’s universities from global academic networks;

blockade driven procurement barriers that delayed or blocked laboratory equipment, reagents, and IT infrastructure, eroding research capacity and maintenance of scientific facilities; 

economic attrition that pushed universities into structural fragility (tuition dependency amid mass impoverishment), weakening staffing, infrastructure upkeep, and student support;

repeated military assaults (notably 2008–2009 and 2014) that damaged or destroyed university buildings and laboratories, long before 2023—Islamic University of Gaza and other campuses were repeatedly struck (United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs 2025);

surveillance and permit regimes that constrained academic freedom and everyday life, embedding the university within broader architectures of security governance and control over movement, assembly, and pedagogy (de Sousa Santos 2015).

Several outlets and human rights reporting confirm repeated strikes on university campuses prior to 2023; however, I found no authoritative UN or academic source substantiating the precise claim that “over 75 laboratories were destroyed between 2008 and 2014.” To remain fully evidence based, I reference the documented bombardments of 2008–2009 and 2014, which did damage laboratories and teaching facilities, without asserting an unverified count (United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs 2025)

In sum, the post 2023 annihilation escalated a structural continuum of scholasticide: a long running matrix of siege, surveillance, economic constriction, and punctuated military destruction that cumulatively eroded intellectual sovereignty and institutional resilience. 

<h4>Scholasticide as Future Oriented Violence</h4>

Scholasticide’s effects are intergenerational and systemic, targeting not only present populations but the very possibility of a sovereign, functional society. Its long term structural consequences include:

<ul class="dashlist">
	
	<li><b>Brain drain and loss of professional classes.</b> The killing of academics and the collapse of campuses sever intellectual lineages and spur out migration of surviving talent.</li>
	
	<li><b>Governance incapacity.</b> Higher education is the pipeline for administrators, civil servants, jurists, and planners; its destruction strips the state of human capital essential to institutional recovery and rule of law consolidation.</li>
	
	<li><b>Collapse of medical and technical sectors.</b> The destruction of medical schools in Gaza and the large scale loss of health workers leave no training pipeline to replenish doctors and nurses, compounding a health system already operating with partially functional hospitals and attrited staff.</li>
	
	<li><b>Economic stagnation.</b> Universities generate skills and research that drive productivity; eliminating these engines depresses growth, employability, and reconstruction capacity for years to come.</li>
	
	<li><b>Radicalization vulnerability.</b> In the absence of educational and professional pathways, youth precarity deepens, a risk highlighted in policy analyses linking higher education to social stabilization.</li>
	
	<li><b>Institutional amnesia.</b> The destruction of archives, libraries, and museums obliterates the repositories of memory that anchor curricular continuity and collective identity.</li>
	
	<li><b>Intergenerational trauma and dependency.</b> With schools destroyed or converted to shelters, children face prolonged educational deprivation and psychosocial distress, undermining social cohesion and creating generational dependency.</li>
	
	<li><b>Post conflict instability.</b> Without engineers, architects, and planners—all products of higher education—reconstruction stalls, leaving material devastation unrepaired and prolonging humanitarian crises.</li>

</ul>

Through these cumulative effects, scholasticide reverses education’s social function: from a mechanism of development and mobility to a mechanism of social extinction, by severing knowledge reproduction, professional formation, and the institutional memory essential to a society’s future.

<h2 class="subtitle">4. Responding to Scholasticide: A De Scholasticide Framework</h2>

The de scholasticide framework reframes education as a form of life-saving civilian infrastructure rather than a secondary development concern. It begins from the understanding that scholasticide is not a series of isolated attacks on schools, but a systematic, cumulative, and future-oriented process that erases a society’s capacity to reproduce knowledge, leadership, and institutional continuity. Because this violence is structural and intergenerational, the response must also be structural, comprehensive, and long-term. Short-term humanitarian interventions focused only on access to schooling or psychosocial support are insufficient; what is required is a holistic model that treats knowledge systems as essential to survival, resilience, and societal continuity.

At its core, de-scholasticide shifts education from the margins of post-conflict recovery to the center of emergency humanitarian necessity. It asserts that knowledge must be protected during conflict, preserved through displacement, and rebuilt as a sovereign system capable of sustaining political, social, and economic life. This involves safeguarding educational institutions, archives, and digital learning systems as protected civilian infrastructure, and recognizing educators and students as civilian groups whose protection is a matter of international humanitarian law. At the same time, it demands continuity of learning even under siege through mobile universities, emergency curricula, low-bandwidth digital platforms, and adaptive systems of assessment and credentialing, ensuring that generational rupture does not become permanent.

The framework also emphasizes preservation as a survival imperative. Intellectual capital, faculty networks, research archives, and academic records must be protected through scholar evacuation pathways, global protection networks, fellowships, and secure digital backups. Reconstruction, in this model, is not limited to rebuilding physical campuses but focuses on regenerating entire institutional ecosystems: governance structures, accreditation bodies, academic autonomy, research systems, and professional pipelines. Without this institutional regeneration, rebuilding produces dependency rather than sovereignty.

Accountability forms another core pillar. The destruction of education is reframed as a legally prosecutable violation of humanitarian norms, requiring systematic documentation, legal advocacy, and international monitoring. At the global level, the framework calls for a shift from charitable assistance to structured academic solidarity, where education becomes a shared transnational responsibility through institutional partnerships, shared curricula, joint degrees, faculty and student exchanges, and coordinated funding for rebuilding knowledge systems. At the community level, it centers local agency by supporting teachers, families, and students as active participants in sustaining education through informal learning networks, peer tutoring, and trauma-informed local pedagogy, rejecting the portrayal of society as merely passive victim.

Within conditions of collapse, Gaza’s academic community has developed what can be described as pedagogies of survival—tent-based PhD defenses, teaching from shelters, WhatsApp classrooms, and virtual learning through unstable connections. These practices demonstrate extraordinary resilience, but the framework insists that resilience alone cannot substitute for structural capacity. Survival strategies, without material, technological, and institutional support, lead to exhaustion rather than sustainability. De scholasticide therefore demands a transition from symbolic solidarity to infrastructural solidarity, transforming survival practices into durable educational pathways.

Looking beyond immediate crisis, the framework envisions a “Day After” strategy that treats knowledge as civilian capital. Recovery is impossible without rebuilding the human and institutional ecosystems that sustain healthcare, governance, engineering, and civil administration. This requires robust digital infrastructure, visiting-student frameworks that allow displaced learners to continue their education abroad, large-scale knowledge preservation through digitization of archives and records, and, critically, institutional sovereignty so that reconstruction results in Palestinian-led governance rather than external dependency.

Underlying this model are foundational principles: that protecting civilians means protecting minds, memory, and knowledge systems; that rebuilding infrastructure without rebuilding education produces dependency, not autonomy; that recovery without intellectual regeneration is structurally impossible; that peace without education is unsustainable; and that reconstruction without knowledge continuity is merely symbolic.

In this sense, de scholasticide is not simply a technical policy framework. It represents a civilizational commitment to protecting knowledge, preserving memory, and defending humanity’s capacity to imagine and construct a future beyond violence. Where scholasticide transforms crisis into permanent collapse by destroying the conditions of future possibility, de scholasticide transforms survival into future-making—restoring continuity, rebuilding generational pathways, and reconstructing the intellectual, cultural, and institutional foundations necessary for sustainable peace, dignity, and self-determination.

<h2 class="subtitle">5. Conclusion: From Survival to Future Making</h2>

This article has conceptualized scholasticide as a structural, cumulative, and future-oriented modality of violence that targets the ecology of knowledge itself. Rather than treating attacks on education as incidental byproducts of conflict, it has argued that scholasticide operates through the systematic destruction of institutions, infrastructures, intellectual communities, and intergenerational transmission, thereby dismantling the conditions through which societies reproduce themselves over time. Read through the lenses of structural violence, infrastructural violence, and slow violence, scholasticide transforms education from a vehicle of mobility and development into a terrain of survival and long-term extinction. In Gaza, this process has entailed not only physical devastation but the erosion of intellectual sovereignty, the fragmentation of professional and social pipelines, and the collapse of collective futurity.

Methodologically, the paper advances a reflexive conceptual–theoretical approach that integrates lived experience with critical social theory to generate an analytic grammar for naming scholasticide as epistemic and civilizational violence. Rather than privileging quantitative datasets, it foregrounds reflexive testimony as epistemically productive, illuminating dimensions of structural harm often obscured by technocratic humanitarian discourse. On this basis, the article articulates de scholasticide as a normative and practical counter-architecture that repositions education as life-saving civilian infrastructure and reframes protection as extending beyond bodies to minds, memory, and knowledge systems.

The de-scholasticide framework advances a comprehensive response model encompassing protection, continuity, preservation, reconstruction, accountability, international responsibility, and community resilience. Together, these dimensions move the response to educational destruction from fragmented humanitarianism toward structural transformation, shifting global engagement from charity to solidarity and from aid dependence to knowledge sovereignty. The article further advances a civilizational claim: knowledge constitutes civilian capital, and its destruction is not collateral damage but an assault on the conditions of future existence. In this sense, scholasticide must be understood as both a mechanism and an indicator of genocidal processes that target a people’s institutional and cultural life.

Ultimately, scholasticide converts temporary crisis into permanent collapse by severing the continuity between past and future. De scholasticide represents its inverse: a commitment to transform survival into future-making through the protection of knowledge, the preservation of memory, and the reconstruction of the institutional infrastructures through which societies endure and regenerate. Any recovery that rebuilds material infrastructure while neglecting education may achieve the appearance of recovery, but not its conditions of possibility.

This research demonstrates that the destruction of education in conflict is not collateral damage—it is a strategic form of long-term societal destruction. When schools, universities, archives, and academic communities are dismantled, the result is not only interrupted learning but the collapse of a society’s ability to govern, heal, rebuild, and sustain itself. Education is not a secondary development sector; it is life-saving civilian infrastructure and a core pillar of social survival.

Policy responses must therefore move beyond short-term humanitarian access models toward structural protection and long-term system rebuilding. Protecting civilians requires protecting knowledge systems. Educational institutions, digital learning platforms, and archives must be formally recognized and enforced as protected civilian infrastructure under international humanitarian law. Attacks on education must be systematically monitored, documented, and prosecuted as violations of civilian protection norms.

Continuity of education during conflict must be operationalized, not symbolic. This requires funding mobile and tented universities, emergency curricula, low-bandwidth digital platforms, and flexible credentialing systems so that learning does not stop during displacement and siege. At the same time, human capital corridors must be established through scholar protection programs, fellowships, evacuations, and visiting appointments to prevent irreversible loss of intellectual communities and leadership capacity.

Reconstruction must focus on institutions, not only buildings. Governance systems, accreditation bodies, research structures, and academic autonomy must be rebuilt alongside physical infrastructure to prevent dependency and ensure long-term sovereignty. International engagement must shift from ad hoc charity to structured academic solidarity through university partnerships, joint degrees, visiting-student frameworks, shared digital repositories, and coordinated global funding for education system recovery.

Finally, communities must be treated as partners, not beneficiaries. Local teachers, students, and families must be supported as active agents in sustaining education through community-led learning networks and trauma-informed pedagogy.

Recovery without education is not recovery — it is managed collapse. Any reconstruction strategy that rebuilds roads and hospitals while neglecting universities, schools, and archives will produce the appearance of stability without its foundations. Protecting education is not an adjunct to humanitarian response; it is the foundation of sustainable recovery, peace, and self-determination.
